Child Development & Learning

Growing Up Different

What Australian data actually tells us about autism, disability and developmental support in children.

70% more autistic children aged 5–14 in Australia in 2022 than in 2015, the fastest-growing age group in the data.

Miguel Varilla · 2026 · FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2

How Common Is It

More children are being diagnosed with autism every year

Between 2015 and 2022, the number of autistic children in Australia rose sharply. This doesn't necessarily mean autism itself is becoming more common, it more likely reflects earlier diagnosis, broader diagnostic criteria and growing awareness.

Among children aged 5–14, the estimated number of autistic children grew by more than 70% in seven years.

Source: ABS, Survey of Disability, Ageing and Carers, 2022

What actually changes for a child once they're diagnosed? We compared autistic children with other children who have a disability but aren't autistic, across four measures of how school actually goes for them.

Autistic children face consistently higher rates of schooling difficulty and need for support than other children with disability, across every measure we checked.

Source: ABS, Survey of Disability, Ageing and Carers, 2022 (Autism in Australia)

Where It Varies

Where a child lives affects what support they get

Datasets that track autism or ADHD diagnosis by region in Australia do not exist. So the maps below use the Australian Early Development Census (AEDC), a national school-entry check that flags children who are "developmentally vulnerable" on one or more of five domains (physical health, social skills, emotional maturity, language and communication).

This is not an autism or ADHD map. A child can be flagged as developmentally vulnerable for many reasons, including health, disadvantage, or an underlying condition. It is used here because it's the only measure with geography available.

The Northern Territory is far higher than anywhere else: 40.8% of children are developmentally vulnerable, compared with 21–26% in every other state and territory. Click a state to highlight it on the maps below.

Source: Australian Early Development Census, 2024

Zooming into individual local areas shows the state figures hide enormous variation. Some regions inside "average" states are just as vulnerable as anywhere in the NT — and much of that variation tracks remoteness: vulnerability climbs from 22.5% in major cities to 35.8% in remote and very remote communities. Circle size shows how many children were surveyed in that area, so a small, extreme reading doesn't visually outweigh a large, reliable one.

Source: AEDC 2024; centroids from HughParsonage/ABS-data (2016 boundaries, 514 of 544 areas matched); remoteness classification from Regional Australia Institute

But when we map actual autism service access instead (not a proxy), using NDIS participation rates, the pattern flips. The NT, highest on developmental vulnerability, has the lowest rate of autism support in the country. That suggests remote communities may be under-served rather than less affected.

Source: NDIS Data Research, Dec 2024; ABS Estimated Resident Population, Dec 2024

Putting both measures on one map makes the mismatch obvious. The Northern Territory is the only state that's both high on developmental vulnerability and low on autism support access, shown here in the darkest colour on the grid.

Source: Australian Early Development Census, 2024; NDIS Data Research, Dec 2024; ABS Estimated Resident Population, Dec 2024

Who It Affects

Age, sex and co-occurring conditions all change the picture

Autism looks different depending on who you're looking at, and often shows up alongside other conditions rather than on its own.

From age 5 onwards, boys are diagnosed with autism at roughly 2–3 times the rate of girls. Under age 5, the pattern is reversed, which may reflect how much later autism is typically recognised in girls.

Source: ABS, Survey of Disability, Ageing and Carers, 2022

Disability doesn't come in one type. Among Australian children aged 0–14 who have a disability, "learning and understanding" difficulties are the single largest category.

Learning and understanding difficulties affect 6.5% of all children aged 0–14, the single largest disability category at this age.

Source: ABS, Children and Young People with Disability, 2022

These figures cover autistic Australians of all ages, not just children. No Australian data currently breaks this down by age for autism specifically. We include it because these co-occurring conditions typically emerge in childhood and tend to persist, so the pattern is still directly relevant to understanding autism in children.

Autism rarely occurs in isolation. 71% of autistic Australians also report difficulty with learning; 62% report social or behavioural difficulties.

Source: ABS, Survey of Disability, Ageing and Carers, 2022 (Autism in Australia)

What Happens Next

Diagnosis comes late, support is uneven, and access is slowly improving

Whether a condition is caught early or late changes everything that follows. And once a child is identified, whether they actually get help depends on where they live, what kind of help they need, and when they were born.

Intellectual disability is rarely diagnosed at age 0–4, but by age 10–14 it accounts for 6.9% of children. That's most likely diagnosis catching up as children get older, not more children actually being affected. Click a group in the legend to trace it on its own.

Source: AIHW, Australia's Children, 2018

Around 1 in 5 students receive a formal educational adjustment for disability, broadly consistent across government, Catholic and independent schools. Each square represents 1% of students.

Source: AIHW, Australia's Children, 2020 (NCCD)

Among children with disability who need help, it's the practical daily needs like health care and self-care that are least likely to be fully met.

Source: ABS, Children and Young People with Disability, 2022

Boys with disability are almost twice as likely as girls to have their schooling restricted in some way.

Source: AIHW, Australia's Children, 2018

Has access to support actually improved over time? Two genuinely multi-year measures say yes.

The share of eligible young children with an NDIS plan rose from 60% in 2019 to 90% in 2023, with a real dip in 2021.

Source: Productivity Commission, Report on Government Services, 2025

For families who do get a plan, the wait has fallen sharply: a median of 10 days in 2024–25, down from 28–46 days in earlier years. 2023–24 data is missing from the official record, and the age band tracked changed slightly in the most recent year.

Source: Productivity Commission, Report on Government Services, 2025

What this adds up to

More children are being identified earlier, and the system is getting faster at processing them once they are. But where a child lives still does more to determine whether they're seen at all than how severe their needs are.

The Northern Territory has the most developmentally vulnerable children in the country, and the lowest rate of autism support access of any state. If the rest of Australia's progress on earlier diagnosis and faster NDIS plans reached remote communities the same way it's reached the cities, the biggest single improvement in this story probably wouldn't be a new program. It would be making the existing one actually reach everyone it's meant to.
