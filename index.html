Child Development & Learning

Growing Up Different

What Australian data actually tells us about autism, disability and developmental support in children, and where that data runs out.

Miguel · 2026 · FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2

Act One: The Scale of It

More children are being diagnosed with autism every year

Between 2015 and 2022, the number of autistic children in Australia rose sharply. That doesn't necessarily mean autism itself is becoming more common. It more likely reflects earlier diagnosis, broader diagnostic criteria and growing awareness.

Among children aged 5–14, the estimated number of autistic children grew by more than 70% in seven years.

Source: ABS, Survey of Disability, Ageing and Carers, 2022

Diagnosis is only the start of the story. What actually changes for a child once they're diagnosed? We compared autistic children with other children who have a disability but aren't autistic, across four measures of how school actually goes for them.

Autistic children face consistently higher rates of schooling difficulty and need for support than other children with disability, across every measure we checked.

Source: ABS, Survey of Disability, Ageing and Carers, 2022 (Autism in Australia)

Act Two: Where It Varies

Where a child grows up shapes what happens next

No Australian dataset tracks autism or ADHD diagnosis by region. We checked. So the three maps below use the closest available measure: the Australian Early Development Census (AEDC), a national school-entry check that flags children who are "developmentally vulnerable" on one or more of five domains (physical health, social skills, emotional maturity, language and communication).

This is not an autism or ADHD map. A child can be flagged as developmentally vulnerable for many reasons, including health, disadvantage, or an underlying condition. We use it here because it's the only measure with any geography at all, and because the pattern it reveals turns out to matter for the rest of the story.

The Northern Territory stands apart: 40.8% of children are developmentally vulnerable, against 21–26% everywhere else. Click a state to highlight it on the three maps below.

Source: Australian Early Development Census, 2024

Zooming into individual local areas shows the state figures hide enormous variation. Some regions inside "average" states are just as vulnerable as anywhere in the NT.

Source: AEDC 2024; centroids from HughParsonage/ABS-data (2016 boundaries, 514 of 544 areas matched)

Splitting those same local areas by remoteness shows why: vulnerability climbs from 22.5% in major cities to 35.8% in remote and very remote communities.

Source: AEDC 2024; remoteness classification from Regional Australia Institute

Here's the twist: when we map actual autism service access (not a proxy) using NDIS participation rates, the pattern flips. The NT, highest on developmental vulnerability, has the lowest rate of autism support in the country, a sign that remote communities may be under-served rather than less affected.

Source: NDIS Data Research, Dec 2024; ABS Estimated Resident Population, Dec 2024

Act Three: Who It Affects

The same condition looks different depending on who you ask

Age, sex and what else a child is dealing with all change the picture, sometimes sharply.

Boys are diagnosed with autism at roughly 2–3 times the rate of girls, across every age group.

Source: ABS, Survey of Disability, Ageing and Carers, 2022

Disability doesn't come in one type. Among Australian children aged 0–14 who have a disability, "learning and understanding" difficulties are the single largest category.

Learning and understanding difficulties affect 6.5% of children with disability aged 0–14, more than any other category.

Source: ABS, Children and Young People with Disability, 2022

These figures cover autistic Australians of all ages, not just children. No Australian data currently breaks this down by age for autism specifically. We include it because these co-occurring conditions typically emerge in childhood and tend to persist, so the pattern is still directly relevant to understanding autism in children.

Autism rarely occurs in isolation. 71% of autistic Australians also report difficulty with learning; 62% report social or behavioural difficulties.

Source: ABS, Survey of Disability, Ageing and Carers, 2022 (Autism in Australia)

Act Four: What Happens Next

Diagnosis lags, support is uneven, and access is slowly improving

A condition identified early looks very different from one caught late. And once a child is identified, whether they actually get help depends on where they live, what kind of help they need, and when they were born.

Intellectual disability makes up almost none of diagnoses at age 0–4, then balloons to 6.9% by age 10–14. That's a sign of diagnosis catching up with reality as children get older, not a sign that more children are becoming affected. Click a group in the legend to trace it on its own.

Source: AIHW, Australia's Children, 2018

Around 1 in 5 students receive a formal educational adjustment for disability, broadly consistent across government, Catholic and independent schools.

Source: AIHW, Australia's Children, 2020 (NCCD)

Among children with disability who need help, practical daily needs like health care and self-care are the least likely to be fully met, the reverse of what many would expect.

Source: ABS, Children and Young People with Disability, 2022

Boys with disability are almost twice as likely as girls to have their schooling restricted in some way.

Source: AIHW, Australia's Children, 2018

Has access to support actually improved over time? Two genuinely multi-year measures say yes, with real gaps and setbacks along the way, not a smooth upward line.

The share of eligible young children with an NDIS plan rose from 60% in 2019 to 90% in 2023, with a real dip in 2021.

Source: Productivity Commission, Report on Government Services, 2025

For families who do get a plan, the wait has fallen sharply: a median of 10 days in 2024–25, down from 28–46 days in earlier years. 2023–24 data is missing from the official record, and the age band tracked changed slightly in the most recent year.

Source: Productivity Commission, Report on Government Services, 2025
